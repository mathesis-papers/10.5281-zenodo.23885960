Reflection-symmetric diagonal synthesis: resolving a conjecture of Huang, Kosugi, Nishi and Matsushita

Haobo Ma, Wenlin Zhang

Paper

How many controlled bit-flip gates are unavoidable when a quantum operation changes phases but treats each bit string like its bitwise complement? Ma and Zhang identify a demanding example and prove a lower bound matching an earlier construction by Huang, Kosugi, Nishi and Matsushita. Together, these results determine the exact worst-case gate count under the manuscript’s restrictions.

A diagonal operation multiplies each computational basis state, labelled by a binary string, by a complex phase without changing the string. Reflection symmetry means that complementing every bit leaves that phase unchanged. Allowed circuits use only CNOT gates, which conditionally flip a target bit, and single-qubit R_z rotations, which give zero and one opposite phases. Implementation must be exact, without extra qubits or measurements, but may differ by a common overall phase. For 2n qubits, Huang and colleagues constructed circuits using 2^(2n−1) + 2n − 2 CNOTs and conjectured this worst-case allowance was necessary. Rotations are not counted.

The authors’ difficult target gives the all-zero and all-one states phase i, a quarter turn, and every other state phase one. Complementation exchanges the marked strings, ensuring symmetry. For two qubits, its phases are i, 1, 1, i. One CNOT computes the inputs’ exclusive-or on a wire; a suitable rotation distinguishes equal from unequal bits, and another CNOT restores the inputs. This implements the target up to overall phase with two CNOTs, matching the lower bound. Not every symmetric operation is difficult: the identity needs none.

The proof tracks which subset of input bits contributes, through exclusive-or, to each wire. This subset is its parity label. CNOT replaces the target label by its exclusive-or with the control label; rotations leave labels unchanged. The labels always describe an invertible binary linear transformation. Since the desired operation changes no basis string, this transformation must finish at the identity. Thus useful labels must appear for rotations, but the original labels must also be restored. This uses the identity-return parity-network viewpoint studied by Amy, Azimzadeh and Mosca.

To force labels to appear, fix a nonempty subset. Multiply the operation’s phases over inputs where that subset’s exclusive-or is zero, then divide by the product where it is one. Overall phase cancels. A rotation using any different label also cancels, by pairing inputs with equal rotation factors but opposite positions in the ratio. For the difficult target, every even-sized subset gives ratio −1: both marked strings contribute i in the numerator. Without a rotation on that label, the ratio would instead be one. Therefore every nonempty even-sized label is required. This multiplicative reasoning avoids ambiguity from adding whole turns to phase angles.

Write m = 2n. There are 2^(m−1) − 1 required labels, each first produced by a CNOT because initial labels select single inputs. Further gates are necessary for restoration. A “birth” combines two odd-sized labels to produce an even-sized target. Every even-sized label ever produced lies in the binary linear span of birth outputs: combining even labels stays in that span, and mixed-size combinations produce odd labels. All required labels together span a space of dimension m−1, forcing at least m−1 births.

Each birth increases the number of currently even-sized labels. Only an update with an even-sized target and odd-sized control decreases it; call this a return. Since that number starts and ends at zero, returns equal births. Returns have odd-sized outputs, so they are additional to the previously counted even-output gates. Hence at least 2^(m−1) + m − 2 CNOTs are required, matching the earlier construction. On four wires this gives ten; Cao and colleagues’ graph-based bound for two-input labels gives nine, while this target also forces the four-input label.

The manuscript gives an all-n written proof and credits the lower bound already present in the pinned Trureturing sources. The authors identify Lean evidence for the lower bound, not the combined worst-case equality, which also uses the literature upper bound. They report exact shortest-network searches only for two and four wires, with lengths two and ten, and character-product and phase checks for two, four, six and eight wires. These finite checks are not the general proof. Replacing n by dn gives the full complement-symmetric class’s count on 2dn wires. Neither optimal depth, where gates may run in parallel, nor optimality within Huang and colleagues’ narrower radial interaction-potential family follows; those questions remain unresolved by this argument.
